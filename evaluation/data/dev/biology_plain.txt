Today we will look at how cells obtain and use energy. Energy enters most ecosystems as sunlight, and plants capture it through photosynthesis. Photosynthesis takes place in the chloroplast, an organelle that contains the green pigment chlorophyll. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy, and the cell uses that energy to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen. The light reactions occur in the thylakoid membranes and produce ATP and NADPH. The Calvin cycle then uses ATP and NADPH to build glucose from carbon dioxide. Cells release the energy stored in glucose through cellular respiration. Cellular respiration takes place mainly in the mitochondrion, which is often called the powerhouse of the cell. Glycolysis is the first stage of respiration, and it splits one molecule of glucose into two molecules of pyruvate in the cytoplasm. The citric acid cycle then breaks down pyruvate and releases carbon dioxide. The electron transport chain, located in the inner mitochondrial membrane, produces most of the ATP. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor of the electron transport chain, and without oxygen the chain stops. ATP is the main energy currency of the cell. ATP stores energy in its phosphate bonds, and when a cell breaks the last phosphate bond the released energy powers work such as muscle contraction and active transport. Enzymes are proteins that speed up chemical reactions by lowering the activation energy. Each enzyme binds a specific substrate at its active site. High temperature or extreme pH can denature an enzyme and destroy its function.
